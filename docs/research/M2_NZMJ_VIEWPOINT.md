# Tobacco wastewater testing and the value of narrowing policy disagreement in Aotearoa New Zealand

Debate about tobacco regulation contains disagreements about what policies would
cause and judgments about which consequences matter. These are different sources
of disagreement. Wastewater testing could help address some of the first kind.
A decision model could then estimate whether the resulting information would be
valuable enough to justify its costs. Neither proposition means that a chemical
measurement can settle an ideological dispute.

The suspension and repeal of the approved smoked-tobacco retailer scheme show
that the policy setting changed; they do not measure the extent or causes of
ideological division.¹ This Viewpoint proposes a way to examine the role of
measurement in a contested debate. It does not diagnose the motives of particular
political groups or report an intervention that has reduced disagreement.

## A measurable contribution to a contested question

One potential source of factual disagreement is the relationship between recorded
lawful tobacco supply and actual tobacco consumption. Falling lawful supply could
reflect less smoking, but interpreting it also requires attention to stocks,
unrecorded lawful channels, population coverage and substitution. Aggregate
wastewater measurements offer an additional source of information about substance
use in a catchment. They do not depend on each person declaring their consumption.

Nicotine exposure is not equivalent to cigarette smoking: nicotine can come from
cigarettes, vaping and therapeutic products. Tobacco-associated markers such as
anabasine may add information, and Australian work has investigated their use in
estimating cigarette consumption from wastewater.² More recent human excretion
research illustrates why conversion factors and product specificity require
careful qualification.³ Urinary results cannot simply be treated as validated
raw-sewage measurement performance in New Zealand.

A marker does not identify whether a cigarette was lawfully supplied. Inferring
unrecorded tobacco consumption would require a matched accounting comparison,
with compatible product definitions, dates, populations and units. Assay recovery,
sewer losses, excretion variability and shared calibration uncertainty also affect
what can be inferred. Greater analytical precision cannot remove an error in
that comparison's population or causal interpretation.

Likewise, a change in a wastewater signal does not by itself identify the effect
of a retail restriction. Other influences on consumption must be considered,
and a credible counterfactual is needed. The repealed retailer scheme cannot be
treated as an implemented national experiment.¹ Wastewater could contribute to
such research, but it is not a substitute for its design.

## What kind of divide could testing narrow?

Evidence could reduce disagreement about a specified quantity if the measurement
is informative, interpretable and accepted as relevant. It cannot decide how to
trade health gains against resource use, liberty, criminal harms or distributional
fairness. These judgments should remain visible rather than being hidden inside
a claim that the data dictate a policy.

| Source of disagreement | Possible contribution of wastewater evidence | What remains necessary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How much tobacco is consumed? | Additional catchment-level measurement | Qualified markers, denominators and uncertainty |
| How much consumption is outside recorded lawful supply? | One input to a matched consumption/supply comparison | Stock/channel adjustments, product coverage and dependence |
| What would a retail restriction cause? | A potential outcome measure | A credible counterfactual and causal design |
| Which consequences should determine policy? | Evidence can inform predicted consequences | Explicit values, rights and distributional judgments |

proposed roles for evidence, not established performance of a New Zealand assay.

Acceptance of a result is also a separate question. Experiments outside New Zealand
have studied politically congruent interpretation of numerical evidence, with
conflicting findings about whether higher numeracy amplifies that tendency.⁴˒⁵
They do not estimate how New Zealand tobacco-policy participants would respond
to wastewater results. It would be unjustified either to assume that publication
of a result produces agreement or to conclude that evidence cannot help.

A useful starting point is to state the factual propositions on which a decision
turns and specify which findings would change the preferred action. Different
participants may revise their factual beliefs and still favour different policies.
Conversely, a practical policy agreement need not require agreement on every fact.
The relevant outcome is a better justified decision, not agreement as an end in itself.

## Estimating whether better information would be worth obtaining

Decision analysis connects information to consequences. For each policy option,
a model represents uncertain outcomes and evaluates them using an explicit
objective. It identifies the preferred action with current information, then
considers the reports that a proposed study might produce. The expected value
of sample information is the average improvement from choosing an action after
the report, compared with the best action chosen now.⁶˒⁷ The model must include
a no-study option and allow a null policy effect or an uninformative test.

This makes the original proposal assessable: testing could be worthwhile when
it is likely to change decisions in ways that improve consequences enough to
justify research costs and any delay. A highly precise test can have little
decision value if it concerns a quantity that does not affect the choice. A less
precise measurement can have greater value if it distinguishes states requiring
different actions. Information gain and policy value are therefore separate criteria.

A companion manuscript, *Value-sensitive research priorities for tobacco regulation:
a conditional decision analysis* (unpublished), applies this reasoning to synthetic tobacco-policy choices. It examines how policy and
research priorities depend on values, uncertain consequences and hypothetical
report performance. It does not fit an empirical New Zealand likelihood or model
political actors. Its numerical results belong to that research article; this
Viewpoint develops the proposal connecting measurement, disagreement and decisions.

The distinction is consequential. Expected information value under a stated
objective is not an estimate of the social value of ending an ideological divide.
It describes an information-responsive decision-maker using a specified prior
and study model. To estimate actual consequences of narrowing disagreement would
also require a defensible account of how reports affect beliefs, whose decisions
change, what actions follow and which consequences are used to judge improvement.
Agreement around an ineffective action would not be a benefit merely because it
was agreement.

A simple sensitivity analysis can make one missing link explicit. Suppose an
informative report leads to the model's preferred response with probability p,
and otherwise leaves the original action unchanged. If that probability is the
same across reports and states, the expected gross decision benefit is p times
the model's information value. Research is worthwhile under this simplified
scenario only when that benefit exceeds study and delay costs expressed on the
same scale. If uptake depends on the report or on who benefits, it must be
modelled explicitly; the simple multiplier no longer suffices.

This is an analytical illustration, not an estimated New Zealand uptake rate.
Neither actual research costs nor a monetary value of political agreement has
been identified. The existing model uses normalised scores, which cannot be
compared directly with a laboratory invoice. A positive gross information value
therefore does not establish that a real wastewater programme is worth funding.

## A proportionate conclusion

The original remit remains a plausible research proposition: tobacco wastewater
measurement could narrow some factual uncertainty within a contested policy
question, and decision analysis can estimate the conditional value of that
information. Current work supports the framework; it has not demonstrated an
actual reduction in ideological division or its societal benefit.

The next analytical task is to test whether conclusions survive plausible assay,
accounting, causal and evidence-use assumptions, with value judgments stated
separately. Relevant public evidence can inform those assumptions without
inventing a consensus or relabelling a forecast as an observation. Existing
public data do not yet settle all of them. National aggregate measurements also
do not establish Māori-specific effects or preferences; a numerical sensitivity
analysis cannot substitute for applicable governance or representation.

Wastewater testing is therefore best considered as a potentially useful component
of a decision-linked evidence strategy. Its value depends on what it can identify,
which decisions it could improve and the cost of doing so. That is a more limited,
but testable, proposition than expecting a measurement to resolve ideology.
